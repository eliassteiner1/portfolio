# Introduction

I built this surfboard during the Covid-19 pandemic as a DIY project at home. Since surfing is a sport I'm pretty passionate about, I always wanted to design and build my very own surfboard. All the technical aspects of this board turned out really well and I'm super happy about the visual desing I was able to paint onto the foam blank. It can be surfed perfectly fine. However, as I didn't have a lot of ressources on how to actually shape the board and mostly freestyled it, it's behaviour in the water is very clunky and not super enjoyable. Nevertheless, this project was a super cool experience to learn about how lamination with epoxy and fibreglass works. 

# Process

This is a really quick overview over the steps that were necessary to build the board. There exist a lot of Bolgposts, YouTube videos and tutorials on how to perform the individual steps

## Shaping the Board

Initially I started with a very roughly surfboard-shaped styrofoam blank. I used woodworking tools to quickly remove excess material. Handplanes and sandpaper are really useful to then get most of the precise shapework done. This step is a lot harder than it might seem, as the performance of the board mostly depends on its shape. Since I freehanded most of the shape from memory, the performance didn't turn out great. I'd recommend to buy some templates (printable guides) from a website or use an existing design. Getting this right involves a lot of experience - which I definitely did not have.

## Adding a Design

This step is purely optional, but adds a lot of character to the board. Normally, only minimal designs are printed on very thin paper and are placed on the board loosely just before glassing. To fully personalize the board, I decided to paint an involved design on both side using acrylic spraypaint. 

However, painting the desing directly onto the styrofoam hurts epoxy adhesion and makes the surfboard more prone to delamination. On the flipside, painting the design onto the epoxy layer after glassing, makes the painting very vulnerable to scratching and abrasion.

## Glassing the Board

Covering the board in fibreglass and epoxy is more straightforward. Strictly following any online tutorial was sufficient. The two sides are glassed one after the other to get a fully watertight hull. The resulting rough surface is sanded generously - although ideally no fibreglass is exposed at this point. Then a final sealing coat of epoxy is applied and sanded smooth.

# Result

I have tested the board in Portugal, surfing it a few days. Due to me not havign any shaping knowledge, the behaviour is pretty difficult to control, especially due to the fairly straight and thick nose. However, I'm very happy with all the technical aspects; the board is watertight, durable and the design turned out great!
